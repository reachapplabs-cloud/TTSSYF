## Answer

### Weighing it: aql, evidence and sources

Marwan ibn al-Hakam is a genuinely useful test case because Sunni sources
themselves supply most of the material needed to weigh him, nothing here
depends on Shia-only sourcing. Three separate questions get run together
in casual usage, and it is worth pulling them apart.

**Was he a companion at all?** Classical Sunni scholarship is split.
Some scholars, applying Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani's definition that anyone who
met the Prophet ﷺ during his lifetime while believing in him and died on
that belief counts as a *sahabi*, classify Marwan as a companion because he
was alive, though a young child, during the Prophet's ﷺ final years [R1].
Others classify him as a *Tabi'i* (successor generation) instead, since he
was too young to meaningfully receive or transmit anything directly [R1].
This is a real, openly acknowledged scholarly disagreement, not a
concealment.

**The exile of his father.** The Prophet ﷺ exiled Marwan's father, al-Hakam
ibn Abi al-As, from Medina to Ta'if, reportedly for hostile conduct;
Marwan went into exile with him as a child [R2]. What happened next is
where the sources genuinely disagree: al-Yaqubi's history states Abu Bakr
and Umar both refused al-Hakam's petitions to return, and it was Uthman,
his own nephew, who finally allowed it once he became Caliph, a decision
that drew heavy criticism from other companions at the time [R2]. Al-Tabari's
history, by contrast, states the Prophet ﷺ himself pardoned al-Hakam before
his death [R2]. These are not reconcilable footnotes, they are competing
accounts of who actually lifted the exile, and this dossier has not opened
either history directly to weigh which is better attested.

**Applying Principle 5 here specifically:** whichever account is correct,
what is not in dispute is the sequence that followed. A family exiled by
the Prophet ﷺ himself was rehabilitated by a kinsman who became Caliph;
that family's son then rose to become governor of Medina and, within a
generation, briefly Caliph in his own right (Marwan I, 684-685 CE). That
trajectory, from exile to governate to the caliphate itself, is exactly
the pattern Principle 5 asks to be flagged and checked, not waved past as
a neutral biographical footnote.

**His conduct as governor.** As governor of Medina under Mu'awiya, Marwan
is reported to have cursed Ali from the pulpit and ordered others to do
the same; one report has him instructing the companion Sahl ibn Sa'd to
curse Ali, which Sahl refused [R3]. This is corroborated on both sides:
a Shia-published source describes it as an affirmed historical fact [R3],
and the chapter's own supplied material, sourced as Sunni, states plainly
that "Sunni records state that while serving as governor of Medina under
Mu'awiyah, Marwan frequently used the pulpit to curse Ali ibn Abi Talib."
Later, he is reported to have advised Yazid to send an army against Medina
and Mecca, a campaign that produced the Battle of al-Harrah (63 AH/683 CE),
the sacking of the Prophet's ﷺ own city, and damage to the Ka'bah [R4].

**His standing as a narrator.** Despite all of this, the majority of
classical hadith critics grade Marwan as *thiqah* (reliable) or *saduq*
(truthful) as a transmitter, and Ibn Hajar records two hadith from him in
Sahih al-Bukhari [R5]. Respected early figures, including Sahl ibn Sa'd
himself (the same man who refused Marwan's order to curse Ali) and Urwah
ibn al-Zubayr, transmitted from him [R5]. This is not a case of Marwan's
political record being hidden from the tradition, it sits right next to
his hadith-reliability rating in the same biographical dictionaries.

### The Shia position, after this research

The strongest form of this argument does not need to claim Sunni
scholarship concealed anything, the opposite is more damning: even with
the exile, the pulpit-cursing of Ali, and the Harrah massacre openly on
the record [R2] [R3] [R4], and even applying Principle 5's flag that his
family's rehabilitation and rise traces directly back to his exiled
father being restored by a kinsman-Caliph, Marwan's hadith transmission is
still graded reliable and preserved in Sahih al-Bukhari [R5]. That is the
"blanket justice" pattern already identified at §4.8 made concrete: a
companion whose recorded conduct includes public hostility to Ali and an
attack on Medina itself is walled off from that conduct the moment the
question shifts to whether his narrations can be trusted.

### The Sunni / Ahl-e-Hadith position, after this research

The fact that all of this is openly documented in Sunni sources is itself
the strongest reply: nothing here was extracted from a hostile outside
source, the companion-vs-Tabi'i dispute [R1], the exile controversy [R2],
and the pulpit-cursing and Harrah advice [R3] [R4] are all recorded and
criticized within the Sunni tradition's own historical literature, which
argues against a charge of whitewashing. *Jarh wa ta'dil* (narrator
criticism) is a narrow technical judgment about a transmitter's precision
and honesty in relaying text, not a global character reference or a
political amnesty, and Sunni scholarship applies that distinction
consistently, not only for Marwan; Sahl ibn Sa'd, who himself refused
Marwan's order, is recorded as still transmitting from him [R5], which is
better read as confidence in his narrational accuracy specifically than
as approval of his politics generally.

### What would resolve this

Direct access to Ibn Hajar's *al-Isabah fi Tamyiz al-Sahabah* and
*Tahdhib al-Tahdhib* to confirm Marwan's exact rijal grading, the specific
wording of the two Bukhari hadith attributed to him, and which named
critics dissented from the *thiqah* rating and why; direct access to
al-Tabari's and al-Yaqubi's histories to weigh which account of al-Hakam's
pardon (by the Prophet ﷺ himself, or only later by Uthman) is better
sourced; and a primary hadith citation, rather than a secondary summary,
for the Sahl ibn Sa'd pulpit-cursing refusal, which is very likely in
Sahih al-Bukhari itself given how often it is referenced.

## Methodology Applied

**Principle 1** is applied to Marwan's *thiqah* rijal grading directly:
rather than accepting "graded reliable by the majority" as closing the
question, this dossier keeps that grading separate from, and does not let
it launder, his separately documented political conduct. **Principle 2**
is applied by extending the same no-exemption standard used elsewhere in
this project (Abu Hurairah, Bukhari) to a companion whose reputation is
already contested rather than celebrated, the point of the principle is
that scrutiny doesn't depend on how famous or beloved the figure is.
**Principle 5** is the central tool here: the sequence from his father's
Prophetic exile, to a kinsman-Caliph's controversial reversal of that
exile, to Marwan's own rise to governate and briefly the caliphate itself,
is exactly the kind of position/wealth/leniency trajectory the money and
power trail heuristic asks to be logged and weighed, not treated as
unrelated biographical facts.
