## Answer

### Weighing it: aql, evidence and sources

This incident, often called the "Calamity of Thursday" (*Raziyyat
al-Khamis*), is one of the most solidly, multiply attested episodes in
Sahih al-Bukhari, appearing in more than one chapter of the collection
[R1], and also in Sahih Muslim [R2]. It is not a fringe or
single-narration claim.

**What the hadith itself records, plainly:** during the Prophet's final
illness, with several companions present, the Prophet asked for writing
materials, in some versions specifically a shoulder blade and inkpot, so
he could write something for the people "after which you would not go
astray." Umar objected, saying, in the commonly cited wording, that
pain had overcome the Prophet and that "we have the Book of Allah, which
is sufficient for us." A dispute followed among those present about
whether to bring the materials anyway, with raised voices, and the
Prophet, on hearing the commotion, said it was not fitting that there be
quarreling in his presence, and the writing was never done [R1]. Ibn
Abbas, present as a young companion and later a major transmitter of
this report, is recorded expressing real grief over it, the "calamity"
is his own characterization, preserved inside Bukhari itself [R3], that
the Prophet was prevented from writing what he wished to write.

**What the hadith does not say, and this matters for precision:** the
text as commonly transmitted does not itself state what the intended
document would have contained. That it would have named Ali as
successor is a widely held inference, argued from context and from
separate material elsewhere in this compilation (Ghadeer Khumm, the
Imamate chapter), not something this specific hadith's own wording
asserts. Stating the inference as if it were the hadith's own content
overreaches what the primary text supports; stating it as the
inference many draw, with its own separate supporting case elsewhere,
is the more precise and defensible framing.

**The core fact that Umar's own words directly countermand an explicit,
present, in-person Prophetic instruction is not seriously disputable**,
it is what the hadith says happened [R1]. What is genuinely disputed is
how to read that fact.

### The Shia position, after this research

The strongest form of this argument does not need the unsupported leap
about the document's contents to land. It is enough, and it is well
attested [R1], that a companion, in the Prophet's own presence, at his
most vulnerable moment, explicitly countermanded a direct instruction on
the stated basis that the Qur'an alone was sufficient, that a dispute
followed in front of the dying Prophet, and that this so troubled Ibn
Abbas, himself a companion and a major transmitter within the Sunni
tradition, that he called it a calamity, a characterization preserved
inside Bukhari itself [R3], not erased or softened by later editors.
That is a serious episode on its own terms, and treating it as such does
not require asserting what the document would have said.

### The Sunni / Ahl-e-Hadith position, after this research

Mainstream Sunni commentary, Ibn Hajar's *Fath al-Bari* and al-Nawawi's
commentary on Muslim among the standard treatments [R4], does not deny
the incident, it offers several charitable explanations for Umar's
objection: concern that the Prophet, weakened by illness, might dictate
something that could later be mistaken for adding to the Qur'an's own
sufficiency; a judgment that the instruction was a preference rather
than a binding command, since the Prophet's own response, asking those
present to stop disputing and leave, rather than insisting the
materials be brought regardless, is read by some classical scholars as
the Prophet himself accepting the outcome rather than being overridden
against his will [R4]. On this reading, Umar's ijtihad, right or
debatable on its merits, was not an act of defiance so much as a
judgment call in a genuinely fraught moment, and the Prophet's own
subsequent words are part of the evidence for how to read it, not
incidental.

## Methodology Applied

**Principle 4** (everything forwarded is context, not fact) is applied
directly to the claim about the document's contents: the inference that
it would have named Ali is treated as an inference to weigh against
separate evidence elsewhere in this compilation, not repeated as if it
were this hadith's own stated content. **Principle 1** is applied to
Umar's action itself: neither "clearly wrong" nor "clearly justified
ijtihad" is treated as self-evident; both readings are stated with their
actual textual basis.
