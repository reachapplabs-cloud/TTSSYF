## Answer

### Weighing it: aql, evidence and sources

**That the practice existed, and existed as policy rather than isolated
incident, is not seriously disputed even in Sunni sources.** Multiple
classical and modern accounts converge on the same basic timeline: public
cursing of Ali from Umayyad pulpits was standard practice from
Muawiyah's establishment of the caliphate (41 AH) until it was formally
abolished roughly sixty years later by Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz, who
instructed his governors to recite Qur'an 16:90 ("Allah commands justice
and good conduct...") in place of the curse during Friday sermons [R1].
The fact that a later, widely-respected Umayyad caliph felt the need to
issue an empire-wide order abolishing the practice is itself strong
indirect evidence that it was institutional, not the private grudge of
a handful of provincial governors acting alone. This is a point where
Sunni and Shia sourcing agree on the underlying fact and differ mainly
on how much weight and moral framing to attach to it.

**The strongest direct evidence for Muawiyah personally ordering it,
rather than merely tolerating it locally, is the well-known Sahih Muslim
report about Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas.** In it, Muawiyah, having appointed
Sa'd governor, asks him directly what stops him from "rebuking Abu
Turab" (Ali's kunya), and Sa'd answers by listing three things the
Prophet said about Ali that make him unwilling to do so [R2]. Sunni and
Shia readings of this same passage diverge sharply on what the question
itself implies: read plainly, Muawiyah asking a newly appointed governor
why he isn't cursing Ali strongly implies cursing Ali was the expected
norm among his governors, something Sa'd was conspicuously not doing.
Some Sunni commentary pushes back on this reading, arguing Muawiyah's
question doesn't necessarily mean he had ordered it as policy, only that
he was registering surprise at Sa'd's abstention from something others
were doing [R2]. That is a real distinction, but it is a thin one: if
"others were doing it" and Muawiyah is surprised Sa'd is the exception,
the practice's normalcy under his governorship is already conceded, only
the precise mechanism (direct written order vs. tolerated expectation)
remains contested.

**A specific attributed letter strengthens the case for explicit
policy, but with a caveat about how it was actually used.** Ibn Abi'l-
Hadid's *Sharh Nahj al-Balagha*, a Mu'tazili commentary that draws on
both Sunni and Shia historical material, is cited as preserving a letter
in which Muawiyah instructs his governors that "hadith about Uthman" be
spread in every city while no one should be permitted to narrate
anything in Ali's favor "without bringing something contradicting this
about the Companions" [R3]. As transmitted in the search results this
dossier could retrieve, this letter is described primarily in terms of
suppressing pro-Ali hadith and promoting pro-Uthman material, and
separately, the same body of literature also attributes to Muawiyah the
direct command to curse Ali on the minbars [R3]. These may be the same
letter described two different ways, or two related but distinct
policies; this dossier has not opened Ibn Abi'l-Hadid's text directly
this session to see whether the cursing instruction and the
hadith-suppression instruction appear in the same document or are
separate reports being run together in secondary summaries.

**The Prophet's own recorded exception is directly relevant and worth
naming plainly.** At Siffin, with Muawiyah's forces actively fighting
Ali, Ali himself is reported to have explicitly forbidden his own men
from cursing Muawiyah's side in return [R4]. If accurate, that creates a
genuine asymmetry worth sitting with rather than glossing over: on one
side of this conflict, cursing the opposing leader from the pulpit
became institutionalized state policy for six decades; on the other,
the leader being cursed is reported to have actively suppressed the
same practice among his own men even at the height of open war. This
dossier has not independently verified the Siffin report's isnad this
session, but it is a widely enough repeated claim in Shia and neutral
historical literature that it belongs in the weighing, flagged with its
confidence level rather than omitted.

### The Shia position, after this research

The strongest form of this argument doesn't need every detail of Ibn
Abi'l-Hadid's letter confirmed to hold. It needs the Sahih Muslim report
[R2], which is itself a Sunni source, to establish that cursing Ali was
the expected norm among Muawiyah's governors, plus the well-attested
sixty-year duration of the practice and its formal abolition by Umar ibn
Abd al-Aziz [R1], which only makes sense as a response to entrenched
institutional practice, not a scattered local habit. From there, the
argument is that a companion so consistently celebrated in later Sunni
memory instituted, or at minimum systematically tolerated and normalized
across his governors, public cursing of the Prophet's cousin, son-in-law,
and (in the Shia reading) rightful successor, for a period spanning most
of a generation, a fact that sits uneasily beside any framing of
Muawiyah as simply another sincere, well-intentioned companion.

### The Sunni / Ahl-e-Hadith position, after this research

The Sahih Muslim report [R2] is real and is not evaded, but its precise
implication is more limited than "Muawiyah personally invented and
ordered this practice everywhere, always." What it shows directly is
that cursing was occurring, or expected, in at least this one governor's
jurisdiction, and that Muawiyah did not object to Sa'd's answer once
given, rather than a documented universal written directive from
Muawiyah himself reaching every province from day one. Classical
commentators including al-Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir are reported to hold
that there is no fully reliable, direct report of Muawiyah personally
cursing Ali or explicitly commanding it in his own words, as opposed to
the practice existing and being tolerated under his and his successors'
rule more broadly [R2]. On the deeper question of moral responsibility,
the practice's abolition by Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz [R1] is itself cited by
Sunni scholarship as proof the tradition self-corrected, a righteous
later ruler recognized the wrong and ended it, which is offered as
evidence the practice was always understood within the tradition as
wrong rather than theologically endorsed.

### What would resolve this

Direct access to Ibn Abi'l-Hadid's *Sharh Nahj al-Balagha* to read the
letter attributed to Muawiyah in full and confirm whether the
hadith-suppression instruction and the cursing instruction are the same
document [R3]; the exact hadith number and full isnad for the Sa'd ibn
Abi Waqqas report in Sahih Muslim, checked directly rather than via
secondary summary, since its precise wording is what the "was this
policy or expectation" question turns on [R2]; and a primary source for
Ali's reported prohibition on cursing Muawiyah's side at Siffin, since
this dossier currently holds that claim at low-medium confidence only
[R4].

**Limitation, stated plainly:** this dossier's account of both the Sahih
Muslim hadith's exact wording and Ibn Abi'l-Hadid's letter rests on
search-result summaries rather than directly opened primary texts. Given
that this question turns substantially on precise wording (does the
Sahih Muslim report show explicit command or tolerated norm; does Ibn
Abi'l-Hadid's letter explicitly mention cursing or only narration
suppression), that gap matters more here than in some other dossiers in
this project, and is reflected in the medium, not high, confidence tags
above.

## Methodology Applied

**Principle 2** applies directly: this dossier does not extend Muawiyah
a pass on the strength of his reputation as a companion and a figure
central to later Sunni political legitimacy, the Sahih Muslim report is
weighed on its own textual implications rather than assumed innocent
because it appears in a trusted collection discussing a widely-revered
figure. **Principle 5** (the money/power trail) is relevant context
though not fully worked here: cursing Ali from every pulpit in the
empire for six decades is, among other things, a state propaganda
apparatus with obvious political benefit to the ruling dynasty's
legitimacy, a pattern worth flagging alongside the narrower
narration-suppression letter [R3], though this dossier does not attempt
the full money/power case study that belongs at §4.13. **Principle 1**
applies to how the Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas report is read: this dossier
treats both the plain reading (cursing was expected norm) and the
minimizing Sunni reading (surprise, not command) as live, contested
interpretations of the same text rather than picking one and treating it
as self-evident.
