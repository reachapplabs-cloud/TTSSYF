## Answer

### Weighing it — aql, evidence & sources

This question actually has two separable layers, and conflating them is
where a lot of the heat in this debate comes from: what Qur'an 9:100
itself says, and the separate methodological doctrine (*al-'adalah*,
the presumed uprightness of the companions as a transmitting class)
that later scholarship built on top of it and similar verses.

**On the verse's own text:** Qur'an 9:100 praises "the forerunners among
the Muhajirun and the Ansar, and those who followed them with *ihsan*
(good conduct)" [R1]. Classical tafsir genuinely disagrees on how far
the *ihsan* qualifier reaches, whether it modifies only "those who
followed," making the forerunners' praise unconditional and the later
followers' praise conditional, or whether continued good conduct is
implicitly the basis for the whole verse's commendation. This is not a
Shia invention: it is a real grammatical and exegetical question
classical commentators discuss and disagree on [R2]. The Shia reading
presented in the source material, that divine pleasure tracks
"lifelong righteous action and continuous faith" rather than a fixed
historical accident of presence, is a defensible reading of that
disagreement, not an ad hoc dodge invented to attack specific
companions.

**On "blanket justice":** what the question is really objecting to is
less the verse itself and more *al-'adalah*, the later methodological
doctrine that treats the companions collectively as reliable
transmitters, exempting them from the individual-by-individual
reliability scrutiny applied to later narrators [R3]. That doctrine
rests on more than 9:100 alone (cumulative praise-verses across the
Qur'an, plus the argument that Islam's entire transmitted record runs
through the companions collectively, so wholesale distrust would
undercut the sources of Islam itself), and it answers a narrower
question than the source material's framing suggests: *al-'adalah*, in
classical usage, concerns whether a companion's hadith transmission can
be trusted, not whether their later political or moral conduct is
immune from judgment. Those are two separate questions that popular
usage on both sides tends to collapse into one.

**On Khayr al-Bariyyah (98:7-8):** the verse itself, "those who believe
and do righteous deeds, they are the best of creation," names no one
specifically. The Ali-specific application comes from a separately
transmitted hadith tradition [R4], reportedly appearing even in a
Sunni-compiled exegetical collection (al-Suyuti's *al-Durr al-Manthur*,
a work known for gathering an unusually wide range of exegetical
hadith). A hadith appearing in a compiled collection is not the same as
it being adopted as settled mainstream doctrine by that collection's
own tradition, the same distinction this project's own §1.1 and §2.1
dossiers already apply to other reports. This dossier has not
independently confirmed the exact wording or location of that report.

**On the named "loyal companions" (Salman al-Farsi, Abu Dharr
al-Ghifari, Miqdad ibn al-Aswad, Ammar ibn Yasir):** this is an accurate
reflection of a real, standard grouping within Shia tradition [R5], and
it's worth being precise about the actual selection criterion being
applied: not a neutral, universally agreed virtue standard, but
specifically continued loyalty to Ali and Ahl al-Bayt through the
succession dispute. Stating that plainly is more honest than presenting
the list as if it followed from some criterion both traditions would
independently agree on.

### The Shia position, after this research

The strongest form of this argument doesn't need to claim the verse is
being misused, it needs only the more modest and textually defensible
claim that *ihsan* in 9:100 carries a real ongoing-conduct component
[R1] [R2], and that a companion whose later actions broke faith with
that conduct, opposing the Prophet's family, cursing Ali from the
pulpit, waging war against the rightful caliph, forfeits the
protection of a verse whose praise depends on continuity in the first
place. This isn't "no standard applies to companions," it's a specific,
coherent standard (continuous *ihsan*) applied consistently, including
to companions Sunni tradition also holds in high regard.

### The Sunni / Ahl-e-Hadith position, after this research

*Al-'adalah* is a real, independently-arrived methodological doctrine
[R3], not invented case-by-case to shield whichever companion is under
attack; it rests on cumulative textual grounds beyond 9:100 alone, and
the practical argument that the transmitted record of Islam depends on
trusting the companions collectively as narrators has real force. But
the doctrine's actual scope is narrower than "blanket justice" implies:
it concerns transmission-reliability, not immunity from moral or
political judgment. Classical Sunni scholarship does criticize specific
companions' later political conduct (Marwan's cursing of Ali is treated
directly, not excused, elsewhere in this compilation at §4.9) while
still accepting their hadith transmission on separate methodological
grounds. The two questions, is this person's narration reliable, and
was this person's later conduct right, are kept conceptually distinct
in careful classical scholarship even when popular usage on both sides
blurs them together.

### What would resolve this

Direct access to classical tafsir on the exact grammatical scope of
*ihsan* in 9:100 (al-Tabari, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi) to confirm how
genuinely divided the classical opinion is rather than this dossier's
general characterization; direct access to *al-Durr al-Manthur* to
confirm the Khayr al-Bariyyah report's exact wording and location; and
a primary source for the classical statement of *al-'adalah*'s actual
scope (a usul al-hadith text) to confirm the transmission-reliability
versus moral-judgment distinction as classically stated rather than as
this dossier's own reconstruction of it.

## Methodology Applied

**Principle 1** is applied by separating the verse's own text from the
later doctrine built on it, and by refusing to treat either "the verse
proves blanket immunity" or "the verse proves nothing" as self-evident.
**Principle 4** is applied to the Khayr al-Bariyyah hadith specifically:
its appearance in a compiled collection is treated as a lead to check,
not an adopted fact, consistent with how this project treats every
other hadith whose presence in a major collection is cited as if it
settled the question of its own authenticity.
