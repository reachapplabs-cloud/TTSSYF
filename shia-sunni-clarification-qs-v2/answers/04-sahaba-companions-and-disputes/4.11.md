## Answer

### Weighing it: aql, evidence and sources

The chapter's own commentary already flags the claimed meanings as the
contributor's own etymological argument, not a cited classical source,
and that caution is warranted, though the underlying etymology itself
turns out to be more defensible than an isolated claim might suggest.

**Muawiya.** Multiple independent Arabic-name reference sources trace the
name to the root *ʿ-w-y*, which in classical Arabic denotes the howling
or crying sound made by canines, specifically associated with female
dogs and the cubs of foxes and other wild animals [R1]. This is a
genuine, attested root meaning, not an invented slur; it appears
consistently across several independent naming-dictionary sources rather
than a single source repeating a claim. What has not been independently
confirmed is a classical lexicon (Lisan al-Arab or Taj al-Arus) opened
directly, or any classical commentary specifically addressing why Abu
Sufyan's household chose this particular name for this particular son
[R1].

**Yazid.** The name derives straightforwardly from the root *z-y-d*, "to
increase" or "to grow," an active-participle meaning "he who increases"
[R2]. This is a far less contested etymology than Muawiya's, it is a
common, plainly positive-sounding Arabic name still in wide use, shared
across history by numerous uncontroversial figures.

**The interpretive leap that needs separating from the etymology.** The
root meaning of Muawiya being genuinely tied to a howling canine does not,
on its own, establish anything about character or destiny. Pre-Islamic
and early Islamic Arab naming convention regularly drew on animal roots,
wolves, lions, dogs, as expressions of toughness, vigilance, or
protectiveness within tribal culture, sometimes even as wards against the
evil eye, rather than as insults at the time of naming. Reading a birth
name chosen in Mecca decades before Muawiya's later political conduct as
a coded prophecy of that conduct is a symbolic, typological argument, not
a linguistic or historical one, and this dossier treats it explicitly as
such rather than repeating it as if the etymology alone proved the point.
This is the chapter's own contributor argument, reproduced per Principle
4 as context to weigh, not fact to adopt.

### The Shia position, after this research

The strongest version of this argument does not overstate the etymology,
it uses the genuinely attested root meaning of Muawiya, howling canine
[R1], as one data point within a broader rhetorical tradition that reads
providence and character into names, particularly for a figure whose
later conduct (ordering Ali cursed from the pulpit, per §4.9's findings)
is independently documented. The argument's force comes from that later
conduct, not from the name predicting it; the name is presented as fitting
retrospectively, which is a legitimate rhetorical move within religious
literature but should not be mistaken for causal or predictive evidence.

### The Sunni / Ahl-e-Hadith position, after this research

The etymology, even if accurate, proves nothing about intent or destiny:
animal-root names were a normal feature of pre-Islamic Arab naming
practice across many tribes and families, not a marker reserved for
disfavored figures, and a name chosen in infancy cannot meaningfully be
read as foreshadowing conduct that occurred decades later and was shaped
by specific political circumstances, not by nomenclature. Yazid's name in
particular undercuts any broader "ominous naming" theory, "increase" is
an unambiguously positive root with no negative connotation at all [R2],
so if the theory required both father-and-son names to carry a coherent
sinister meaning, it does not hold up evenly across both cases.

### What would resolve this

Direct consultation of a classical Arabic lexicon, Lisan al-Arab or Taj
al-Arus, for the full semantic range of the root *ʿ-w-y* and any recorded
classical commentary specifically discussing Muawiya's name; and
historical sourcing, Ibn Sa'd's *al-Tabaqat al-Kubra* or an equivalent
early biographical work, for the actual circumstances and stated reasoning
behind the naming within Abu Sufyan's household, which remains completely
unresearched here and is a separate question from the word's dictionary
meaning.

## References

- **R1**: Root *ʿ-w-y* and its association with the name Muawiya, meaning
  the howling/crying sound of canines, particularly female dogs and fox
  or lion cubs. **High confidence**: three independent Arabic-name
  reference sites (wisdomlib, arabikey, quranicnames) converge on this
  root meaning with no contradicting result found; none of these are
  classical lexicons opened directly, so the root meaning is corroborated
  but not independently verified against Lisan al-Arab.
- **R2**: Root *z-y-d* and its association with the name Yazid, meaning
  "to increase" or "he who increases." **High confidence**: this
  is a straightforward, widely and consistently attested etymology with
  no significant variation or contradiction across sources.
