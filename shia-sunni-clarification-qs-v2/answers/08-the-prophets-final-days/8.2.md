## Answer

This message bundles several distinct claims: the pen-and-paper refusal
(covered fully at §4.2, not repeated here), a suggestion that the
Prophet's coherence was questioned in the moment, a reference to
declining treatment for an illness, and a reference to *qisas*
(retaliatory justice). Each is weighed on its own.

### Weighing it: aql, evidence and sources

**On "ask him" / questioning the Prophet's coherence:** some versions of
the Qirtas narrative include a remark from one of those present, on
hearing the Prophet's instruction, along the lines of questioning
whether he should be asked to repeat himself, which later readers have
sometimes taken as implying doubt about his coherence in that moment
[R4]. This dossier cannot confirm from memory that "is he even our
prophet" is a direct quotation from the primary hadith text itself, as
opposed to a paraphrase or gloss of a more ambiguous original line. This
is flagged plainly rather than repeated as if it were a confirmed direct
quotation: what can be said with confidence is that some version of a
remark questioning whether the Prophet should be asked to clarify or
repeat himself does appear in the tradition around this incident; the
specific wording attributed in the question is not confirmed here.

**On declining treatment for an illness:** a separate, genuinely
attested hadith exists in which companions administered medicine to the
Prophet during his final illness against his express wishes while he
was not in a state to refuse it himself, and he expressed displeasure
on realizing it afterward [R2]. This is a real, separate report from
the Qirtas incident, and conflating the two into one continuous
narrative overstates what either individually supports. They are best
treated as two separate data points about the deathbed period, not one
connected sequence.

**On *qisas*:** a separate, well-attested hadith has the Prophet, near
the end of his life, offering to submit to retaliation himself if he
had wronged anyone, inviting any such claim to be settled before his
death [R3]. This is a report about the Prophet's own humility and
desire to die free of unsettled claims against him, not, on its own, a
report about companions' conduct toward him. The source message's
framing, grouping it with the pen-and-paper and treatment episodes as
evidence of mistreatment, stretches what this specific report is
actually about; it does not describe companions imposing anything on
the Prophet.

**On Sahih al-Bukhari 7280, Kitab al-I'tisam bil-Kitab wal-Sunnah:** the
book title given matches a real chapter of Bukhari that does contain a
version of the Qirtas narrative [R1], which is a good corroborating
signal that this citation is genuine and correctly attributed. The
specific number 7280 is not independently confirmed from memory with
full certainty in this session.

### The Shia position, after this research

The strongest, most defensible version of this argument rests on the
Qirtas incident itself, already treated fully at §4.2, and does not
need the additional, looser claims (the qisas reference [R3], the
coherence remark as quoted [R4]) to carry weight. Piling on
less-well-supported claims around a genuinely strong core point weakens
the overall argument rather than strengthening it; a reader who checks
the qisas hadith and finds it describes something else entirely has
reason to discount the rest of the argument too, even where the rest
holds up. The core, well-attested point stands on its own: a direct
instruction was countermanded, in the Prophet's presence, at his most
vulnerable, and a companion who was there called it a calamity.

### The Sunni / Ahl-e-Hadith position, after this research

The mainstream response, beyond what's already covered at §4.2, is that
combining several separate hadith into one continuous narrative of
mistreatment reads more into the sources than any one of them
individually supports. The medicine incident [R2], the qisas offer
[R3], and the Qirtas incident [R1] come from different moments and
different reports, each with its own context, and treating them as one
connected story of abandonment is an interpretive construction laid on
top of the sources, not something the sources themselves present as a
single sequence.

### What would resolve this

Direct access to sunnah.com to confirm hadith 7280's exact text and
whether "is he even our prophet" or an equivalent phrase appears in any
primary version of this narrative, or whether that is a later paraphrase;
and direct access to the medicine-incident and qisas hadith to confirm
they are correctly summarized here as separate reports rather than part
of one continuous account.
