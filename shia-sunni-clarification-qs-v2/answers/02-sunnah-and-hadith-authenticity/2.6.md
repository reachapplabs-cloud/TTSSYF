## Answer

### Weighing it: aql, evidence and sources

The claim has two separable halves, and they hold up very differently.

**Half one: were Shiat-al-Ali the first to compile hadith in writing?**
There is real, specific, named evidence for a version of this claim,
though "first muhaddithin" as a sweeping label overstates what the
evidence actually supports. Multiple sources report that among the
earliest hadith compilers organized by topic, rather than simply
recorded individually, were Salman al-Farisi and Abu Dharr al-Ghifari,
both counted among the earliest and closest companions of Ali; classical
Shia bio-bibliographers al-Tusi and al-Najashi are reported to record
specific titled works attributed to them, Salman's *Kitab Hadith
al-Jathliq* and Abu Dharr's *Kitab al-Khutbah*, with traced chains of
transmission [R1]. Separately, Ibn Shahrashub is reported to hold that
Ali ibn Abi Talib himself, followed by Salman, was first to compile a
book in Islam at all [R2].

But this is not the only serious candidate for "earliest written hadith
compilation" in the historical record, and the honest picture includes
a real competing claim from within Sunni-transmitted material. Sahifah
al-Sadiqah, compiled by the companion Abd Allah ibn Amr ibn al-As with
the Prophet's ﷺ own explicit permission to write during his lifetime,
containing roughly a thousand narrations, is independently and widely
described as "the first book of hadith" in its own right [R3]. That
claim and the Salman/Abu Dharr claim are not necessarily mutually
exclusive, they may describe different senses of "first" (individually
recorded versus topically organized, or compiled during the Prophet's ﷺ
lifetime versus after), but this project has not independently resolved
which, if either, has clearer chronological priority, and presenting the
Shiat-al-Ali claim without this competing candidate would be exactly the
one-sided sourcing this project's protocol exists to prevent.

A further, directly relevant complication: Sunni sources themselves
report that Umar ibn al-Khattab moved to restrict, and in some accounts
actively burn, written hadith collections, stating a fear that they might
compete with or encroach upon the Qur'an, and instructing that any
document bearing a Prophetic tradition be destroyed [R4]. If accurate,
this would mean any written hadith-compilation activity in the earliest
Sunni-aligned circles was actively suppressed by caliphal policy for a
period, which is directly relevant context for judging why the surviving
written record looks the way it does, and is a real example of this
project's Principle 5 (the money/power trail): whoever's material
survived or didn't survive from this period was shaped by a specific
policy decision, not by neutral historical accident alone. This project
has not independently verified the primary hadith text of Umar's
prohibition or its exact scope (a directive against writing generally,
versus specifically writing anything that might be mistaken for or
supplement scripture).

**Half two: does "Ahl-e-Hadith" trace to Qur'anic text, unlike a
supposedly man-made "Ahl as-Sunnah"?** This half does not hold up as
stated. The Qur'an does use the word *hadith* itself, most notably in
39:23's "ahsan al-hadith" ("the best of discourse/speech"), referring to
the Qur'an itself as the best hadith, but the specific compound phrase
"Ahl al-Hadith" does not appear anywhere in the Qur'anic text [R5]. What
does have Qur'anic and Prophetic terminological precedent is the broader
pattern the "Ahl al-" construction belongs to, the Qur'an uses "Ahl
al-Kitab" (People of the Book) and "Ahl al-Injil" (People of the Gospel)
as descriptive labels for other communities, and hadith reports the
Prophet ﷺ addressing memorizers of the Qur'an as "Ahl al-Qur'an" [R6],
so the naming pattern itself has real precedent even though the specific
term "Ahl al-Hadith" is not one of those Qur'anic instances. As a
historical school-identity, "Ahl al-Hadith" is consistently dated by
multiple sources to the 2nd-3rd Islamic centuries (roughly late 8th-9th
century CE), emerging as a movement of scholars insisting the Qur'an and
authenticated hadith were the sole authority in law and creed, developing
partly as a reaction within the community rather than a term with a
direct textual origin claim of its own [R7].

On the parallel claim that "Ahl as-Sunnah" is comparatively man-made: the
term "Ahl as-Sunnah wal Jama'ah" is also reported to be a later,
human-coined label, joined together at the end of the second Islamic
century specifically to distinguish the mainstream from emerging sects,
by some accounts including some of Bukhari's own teachers among the
first to combine the two words, with one account tracing an earlier
political echo to Mu'awiyah's declared "year of Jama'ah" [R8]. If
accurate, both terms, "Ahl al-Hadith" and "Ahl as-Sunnah," would turn out
to be comparably late, human-coined identity labels from roughly the
same historical window, rather than one being textually original and the
other man-made, which undercuts the specific contrast the original claim
draws, even though the "Ahl al-Hadith" side happens to share its literal
vocabulary (the word *hadith*) with a word the Qur'an also uses.

### The Shia position, after this research

The strongest form of this claim isn't the sweeping "first muhaddithin
in Islam, full stop" framing, it's the more specific and better-evidenced
point that named, close companions of Ali, Salman al-Farisi and Abu Dharr
al-Ghifari foremost among them, are independently credited in classical
bio-bibliographical sources with early, topically organized written
hadith works, with Ali himself credited as compiling the very first book
in Islam [R1] [R2]. Read alongside Umar's reported prohibition on writing
hadith more broadly [R4], the Shia case gains real weight: if the
Shiat-al-Ali circle kept writing and preserving material through a period
when official policy discouraged or destroyed such writing elsewhere,
that is a coherent, documentable claim about relative priority, distinct
from and stronger than an unqualified "first ever" claim that a fair
reading of the evidence, including the Sahifah al-Sadiqah counter-example
[R3], does not clearly support.

### The Sunni / Ahl-e-Hadith position, after this research

The counter-evidence here is specific rather than merely defensive: Abd
Allah ibn Amr ibn al-As is independently and widely credited with the
first written hadith compilation, made during the Prophet's ﷺ own
lifetime and with his explicit permission [R3], which is an earlier and
more directly documented starting point than a general claim about the
broader Shiat-al-Ali circle. On etymology, the term "Ahl al-Hadith" does
not actually appear as a phrase in the Qur'an [R5], so the specific
claim that it is Qur'anic in origin does not hold up as stated, even
though the underlying word does. Both "Ahl al-Hadith" and "Ahl as-Sunnah"
are best documented as later, human-coined community labels from a
comparable historical window [R7] [R8], which is a materially different,
and more defensible, position than the original claim's asymmetric
framing (one term textually original, the other man-made) suggests.

### What would resolve this

Direct access to al-Tusi's *Fihrist* and al-Najashi's *Rijal* to confirm
the specific titled works and transmission chains attributed to Salman
al-Farisi and Abu Dharr al-Ghifari, rather than the search-surfaced
secondary summary used here; direct access to a primary source (Ibn
Sa'd's *Tabaqat*, or a hadith-sciences history such as Ibn al-Salah's
*Muqaddimah*) comparing the Sahifah al-Sadiqah's dating against the
Salman/Abu Dharr works to establish actual chronological priority rather
than presenting them as an unresolved tie; direct access to the primary
hadith text(s) reporting Umar's restriction on writing hadith, to confirm
its exact wording and scope; and a primary source (such as a classical
tabaqat or history covering the 2nd-century emergence of sectarian
naming) for the specific claim that some of Bukhari's own teachers were
among the first to combine "Ahl as-Sunnah" and "al-Jama'ah."

## Methodology Applied

**Principle 4** applies most directly here, as flagged in the original
chapter entry itself: the claim is presented, in its own source wording,
as "the take from their side," a claim relayed rather than asserted, and
this dossier's job was to research it rather than adopt or dismiss it on
arrival. The research found the first half (Shiat-al-Ali as early
hadith-compilers) partially supported but overstated as an unqualified
"first" claim once the Sahifah al-Sadiqah counter-example is honestly
weighed, and found the second half (Qur'anic origin of "Ahl-e-Hadith"
specifically) does not hold up as stated. **Principle 5** applies to
Umar's reported hadith-writing prohibition: a caliphal policy decision
that would have shaped whose written material survived from the earliest
period is treated here as a real, worth-investigating factor in why the
Shiat-al-Ali written record looks the way it does relative to
Sunni-transmitted material, rather than a neutral biographical footnote,
though this project has not yet independently verified the prohibition's
primary text or precise scope. **Principle 1** applies to how both
sides' "first" claims are handled symmetrically: neither the Shia claim
to priority nor the Sunni Sahifah al-Sadiqah counter-claim is accepted
as automatically settling the question, both are presented as competing,
partially-evidenced claims.
