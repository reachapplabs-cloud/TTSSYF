## Answer

### Weighing it — aql, evidence & sources

**The mainstream classical reading is genuinely near-universal, not a
minority position being protected.** Al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir, al-Qurtubi,
al-Baydawi, al-Zamakhshari, and effectively every major classical tafsir
identify the Prophet as the one who frowned at the blind companion
'Abdullah ibn Umm Maktum while occupied with prominent Quraysh figures
[R1]. This is not treated in that literature as a moral failing in the
severe sense, it is read through the standard doctrine of *'ismah*,
protection from major sin and doctrinal error, which was never
understood in mainstream Sunni theology to mean total moral perfection
in every minor, non-doctrinal choice [R2]. On the tradition's own terms,
this already resolves the "contradiction" the question raises without
needing to relocate the frowning to anyone else: excellent character and
an occasional less-than-optimal choice, gently corrected, are not
actually in tension under this doctrine.

**The alternative reading (a third, unnamed party frowned, and the
Prophet is only being addressed about it) is a real minority position
in the literature, but this dossier cannot independently confirm its
classical pedigree or prominence from the source material alone.** The
source does not name which scholars hold it, only linking to Al-Islam.org
pages [R3] this project has not independently opened to confirm what
they specifically argue and cite. This is flagged plainly rather than
either dismissed or credited beyond what's actually been checked.

**The grammatical argument for a subject-shift is weaker than it looks,
and this is worth stating directly.** The claim rests on 80:1's
third-person "he frowned" being grammatically distinct from 80:3's
second-person address "you" (*yudrika*), taken as evidence they refer to
different people. But third-to-second-person shifts within a single
passage, addressing the very same subject throughout, are a well
documented, common feature of Qur'anic rhetorical style known as
*iltifat* [R4], used elsewhere in the Qur'an without signaling any
change of referent. A person-shift alone, without further evidence,
doesn't carry the weight this argument assigns it. This doesn't rule
out the alternative reading, but it does mean the grammatical case for
it is thinner than presented.

**On the companions'-infallibility premise:** the source's stated "base"
premise, that companions are not held infallible in mainstream theology,
is accurate and uncontroversial [R2]. But it's a premise that would be
needed only if the frowning party were a companion, it doesn't itself
establish that a companion, rather than the Prophet, is who the passage
actually describes.

### The position defending the alternative reading, after this research

The strongest form of this argument doesn't need to win the grammatical
point decisively, it needs only that *iltifat* alone doesn't rule out a
subject-shift here, and that mainstream theology already accepts
companions are fallible, so there is no doctrinal barrier to reading an
unnamed companion as the one gently corrected. Framed that carefully,
it's a live possibility worth investigating properly, via the specific
classical or modern commentators who hold it, rather than framed as
already-settled from grammar alone.

### The mainstream Sunni position, after this research

The near-universal classical identification of the Prophet as the one
who frowned isn't a position that needs defending against an implied
charge of protecting a companion at his expense, it's simply the
dominant reading across the tafsir tradition [R1], read through a
doctrine of prophetic protection that was never understood to mean
freedom from every minor imperfect choice [R2]. Some classical
commentators go further and treat the episode approvingly, as evidence
of the Qur'an's own honesty and refusal to flatter its Prophet, an
observation sometimes used apologetically as evidence for the text's
authenticity. And the grammatical case for relocating the frowning to
someone else is weaker than presented, since person-shift within one
passage addressing the same subject is common Qur'anic style [R4], not
inherently evidence of a different referent.

### What would resolve this

Direct access to the specific Al-Islam.org pages the source links [R3]
to confirm which scholars, classical or modern, actually hold the
third-party reading and on what basis beyond the grammatical argument
addressed above; and a primary classical source on *iltifat* to confirm
its scope and whether any classical scholar specifically discusses it in
relation to this passage.

## Methodology Applied

**Principle 1** is applied by not treating either "it's the mainstream
reading, so it's correct" or "the grammar proves a subject-shift" as
self-evident: the mainstream reading is stated with its actual doctrinal
basis (R2), and the grammatical argument for the alternative is weighed
against a real countervailing linguistic feature (R4) rather than
accepted or dismissed outright. **Principle 4** is applied to the
source's rhetorical aside about "the ones known to curse" defending the
Prophet's character: preserved as the source's own framing, not treated
as a claim requiring separate technical evaluation.
