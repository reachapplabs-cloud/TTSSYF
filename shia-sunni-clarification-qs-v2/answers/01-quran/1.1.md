## Answer

### Weighing it — aql, evidence & sources

The essay's central move is a fairness/double-standard argument, and it
holds up on its own terms, with one important asymmetry worth being
precise about.

**Is al-Kulayni's report representative of Shia belief?** No, and this
is not a minor technicality. Al-Kulayni's *Usul al-Kafi* records a
report, attributed through its chain to Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, describing
a larger original revelation than the standard 6,236-verse mushaf.
Mainstream Twelver Shia scholarship does not treat this report as
settled doctrine, it is criticized on chain and content grounds by major
Twelver scholars (Grand Ayatollah Abu al-Qasim al-Khoei's *Al-Bayan fi
Tafsir al-Qur'an* is the standard modern reference explicitly defending
the Qur'an's textual completeness against exactly this kind of report),
and official Twelver doctrine, as the essay itself states, holds the
Qur'an complete and unaltered. A hadith collector recording a report is
not the same thing as that report being endorsed as core doctrine by the
tradition that produced the collection, this is the same distinction
this project's own §2.1 dossier just applied to Sahih Muslim recording
the Ka'b al-Ahbar creation report without every later Sunni scholar
treating it as settled Prophetic speech. Applying that same standard
here: singling out "millions of Shi'ites" over one recorded, contested,
minority-held report is not evidentially sound, by the same logic this
project already uses elsewhere.

**Are the Sunni-side reports genuinely comparable?** Largely yes, as
factual reports, but the two traditions handle their respective
difficult reports differently, and that difference matters. The
Umar/stoning-verse account (recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari, part of the
same primary text already reproduced in this chapter) has a specific,
long-standing classical treatment in Sunni legal theory: *naskh
al-tilawah duna al-hukm*, abrogation of a verse's recitation while its
legal ruling remains in force, transmitted onward through practice and
consensus rather than the written text. This is a real, named,
extensively discussed category in classical *usul al-fiqh* (alongside
abrogation of ruling alone, and abrogation of both together), not an ad
hoc dodge invented to explain away this one report. Abu Musa
al-Ash'ari's and Aisha's accounts (forgotten surahs, an abrogated
nursing-verse count) are handled the same way, as recitation that was
later abrogated, not as the current mushaf being incomplete.

So the more precise claim isn't "Sunni sources admit the Qur'an is
missing verses," it's "Sunni sources record reports of verses whose
*recitation* was later abrogated while the current mushaf is still held
complete, exactly the same structure of claim al-Kulayni's report makes
for the Shia side, but the two traditions answer their own version of
this problem differently." Mainstream Twelver scholarship largely
answers by disputing the report's reliability outright; mainstream
Sunni scholarship answers by classifying the specific verses as
abrogated-in-recitation. Both are internally coherent scholarly moves.
Neither tradition is caught flat-footed by its own version of this
report the way an outside accusation assumes.

### The Shia position, after this research

The fairness argument is genuinely strong and gets stronger, not weaker,
once the doctrinal detail is added: Sunni sources report companions
describing verses no longer in the mushaf (Umar on stoning, Abu Musa on
two forgotten surahs, Aisha and Ubay ibn Ka'b on similar material), from
the same collectors, Bukhari, Muslim, Ahmad, held up as the most
authoritative in Sunni Islam. If one recorded, minority-rejected report
in a Shia hadith collection is sufficient grounds to accuse the entire
Shia tradition of believing the Qur'an incomplete, an evenhanded
application of that same standard would require the same conclusion
about Sunni tradition from its own comparable reports, which is not
what actually happens. The double standard is real and worth naming
plainly.

### The Sunni / Ahl-e-Hadith position, after this research

The fair response here isn't to deny the Bukhari/Muslim reports exist,
they do, and pretending otherwise would be its own kind of dishonesty.
It's to point out that Sunni scholarship has a specific, centuries-old,
named doctrinal answer for exactly this category of report
(*naskh al-tilawah*), developed independently of this argument and
applied consistently across all such reports, not invented in response
to this specific challenge. That is a materially different position
than simply having no answer. Whether al-Kulayni's report has an
equally well-developed, equally mainstream doctrinal answer inside
Twelver scholarship, as opposed to being handled by disputing the
report's chain instead, is worth being precise about rather than
treating both traditions' responses as interchangeable.

### What would resolve this

Direct access to the primary Sunni *usul al-fiqh* literature on *naskh
al-tilawah duna al-hukm* to confirm how early and how consistently this
category was applied to these specific reports, rather than relying on
general knowledge of the doctrine; and a direct reading of al-Khoei's
*Al-Bayan* (or an equivalent standard Twelver source) to confirm exactly
how mainstream Shia scholarship treats al-Kulayni's report, rather than
this dossier's general characterization of that position.

## Methodology Applied

**Principle 4** (everything forwarded is context, not fact) is applied
by treating the essay's framing as a strong argument to weigh, not as
an assumed correct conclusion, and by holding both traditions' handling
of their own difficult reports to the same standard rather than
assuming the accusation or the rebuttal is automatically right.
**Principle 1** is applied by not treating either "it's in Bukhari" or
"it's in al-Kafi" as automatically decisive on its own, in either
direction, each report's actual doctrinal treatment inside its own
tradition is what the comparison in this dossier actually rests on.
