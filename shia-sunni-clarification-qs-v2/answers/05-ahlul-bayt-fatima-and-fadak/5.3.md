## Answer

### Weighing it: aql, evidence and sources

The question as posed cannot be answered exactly as asked. The chapter
file itself flags this gap: the source transcript names "this sheikh" and
a page range (89/90) but the book title, author, and any attached file
never made it into the exported text, so this dossier has no specific
Sunni-sheikh account to hold up against Fatima's sermon. That gap is
recorded here rather than papered over with a guess at which book was
meant. What this dossier can do honestly is compare the two accounts that
actually exist and are traceable: the terse, hadith-collection version of
the Fadak episode that Sunni sources including Sahih al-Bukhari carry via
Aisha, against the much longer sermon attributed to Fatima and preserved
mainly in later historical and adab literature, including a notable
Sunni-adjacent source. That comparison is close in spirit to what the
question is really after (which telling is more securely grounded) even
though it cannot be pinned to the specific unnamed sheikh.

**The Bukhari account (via Aisha), already documented at [5.2](5.2.md):**
Fatima sent Abu Bakr a request for her inheritance share, he refused and
cited the "prophets leave no inheritance" hadith, she became angry and
did not speak to him again until she died [R1]. This version is short,
inside a canonical hadith collection, and contains no sermon at all,
Fatima's response in Bukhari's telling is silence and estrangement, not a
public speech.

**Ibn Sa'd's and al-Baladhuri's historical accounts add detail Bukhari
does not carry, and disagree with each other on it.** Ibn Sa'd's
*Tabaqat* has Fatima basing her claim on testimony from Umm Ayman rather
than a direct statement from the Prophet to her; al-Baladhuri's *Ansab
al-Ashraf* instead has Fatima claiming the Prophet gave her Fadak
directly, with Ali and Umm Ayman offering supporting testimony that Abu
Bakr judged insufficient under the normal two-witness (or one-man-two-
women) standard [R2]. Both of these are Sunni-authored historical works,
not hadith collections, and neither is presented as carrying a full
chain matching hadith-critical standards, which is itself worth noting:
this material sits in history/*akhbar* literature, a genre classical
Sunni scholarship generally holds to looser sourcing standards than
hadith proper.

**The sermon itself is preserved at real length, but mainly outside the
Sunni hadith canon, and by writers with mixed lineages.** The fullest
versions circulate through Shia compilations (al-Tabarsi's *al-Ihtijaj*
and similar) and through the Kufa-based historian Abu al-Faraj
al-Isfahani's *Maqatil al-Talibiyyin* [R3]. Notably, a substantial Sunni-
adjacent figure also preserves parts of it: Ibn Abi al-Hadid, a
thirteenth-century Mu'tazili scholar working within a broadly Sunni
intellectual tradition, includes portions of Fatima's sermon in his
*Sharh Nahj al-Balagha*, drawing on earlier historical sources rather
than inventing the material himself [R4]. That a non-Shia commentator
transmits parts of it is real cross-tradition corroboration that some
version of the sermon circulated in the wider historical literature, not
only in confessionally Shia works, though it does not establish a sound
hadith-style chain back to Fatima's own words.

**The honest asymmetry worth stating plainly:** the inheritance-refusal
exchange is attested inside the most rigorously chain-scrutinized Sunni
collection there is, Bukhari, while the sermon is attested at much
greater length but almost entirely in historical/adab genres that were
never subjected to that same chain discipline, on either side. That does
not make the sermon fabricated, plenty of real historical speech never
entered hadith collections because those collections were curated for
legal and doctrinal material, not political oratory, and a full-length
public sermon protesting a caliphal decision is exactly the kind of text
that genre boundary would have excluded regardless of its authenticity.
But it does mean the sermon's transmission has not been tested by the
same isnad-critical apparatus this project applies to hadith, and
presenting it as equally "authenticated" as the Bukhari material would
overstate what has actually been checked here.

### The Shia position, after this research

The core grievance, that Abu Bakr refused Fatima's claim and she was
angered by it, is not something Shia sources have to argue alone, it is
in Bukhari itself [R1]. The sermon then supplies the content and
reasoning Bukhari's terse account omits entirely: Fatima's own stated
argument for why the inheritance ruling did not apply to her, delivered
publicly rather than privately. That a non-Shia scholar (Ibn Abi al-Hadid)
transmits parts of the same sermon independently [R4] is read as evidence
the speech is a real historical event with multiple transmission lines,
not a purely sectarian invention, even though its exact wording cannot be
chain-verified the way a hadith can.

### The Sunni / Ahl-e-Hadith position, after this research

The fair Sunni response does not need to deny the sermon exists in later
literature, it can simply note that its transmission sits outside the
hadith-critical apparatus that verified the inheritance-refusal narration
in Bukhari, and that the earliest and most rigorously sourced version of
this episode (Bukhari's) contains no sermon at all [R1]. Ibn Sa'd and
al-Baladhuri's competing, mutually inconsistent details about what
evidence Fatima presented [R2] are themselves cited as a reason for
caution about the later historical layer generally, if the near-
contemporary historians cannot agree on the details of the claim, a much
longer speech text compiled still later deserves the same scrutiny, not
a pass because it is emotionally compelling.

### What would resolve this

First and most directly: recovering the actual book and page 89/90 the
original question names, this dossier cannot compare "which is authentic"
against a source it has never seen, and that gap should not be quietly
dropped from the project's open items. Beyond that: direct access to
al-Tabarsi's *al-Ihtijaj* and Abu al-Faraj al-Isfahani's *Maqatil
al-Talibiyyin* to check the sermon's earliest textual witnesses and
whatever chain information they carry; a side-by-side read of Ibn Sa'd,
al-Baladhuri, and Bukhari's Fadak narrations to map exactly where they
agree and diverge rather than this dossier's general characterization of
that divergence.

## Methodology Applied

**Principle 3** applies to the genre gap itself: the sermon's near-total
absence from the Sunni hadith canon (as opposed to historical/adab
literature) is noted and weighed rather than silently read as either
proof of fabrication or proof of suppression, both of which would overstate
what the absence actually shows on its own. **Principle 4** applies to the
missing sheikh/book: rather than inventing a plausible-sounding source to
fill the gap, the dossier states the gap and substitutes a comparison it
can actually support. **Principle 1** applies to treating Ibn Sa'd and
al-Baladhuri's accounts as historical material with looser sourcing
standards than hadith, not as equivalent in evidentiary weight to a
chain-scrutinized report, in either direction.
